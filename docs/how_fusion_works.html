How focus stacking works

OpenFocus takes several photos of the same scene, each focused at a different distance, and builds one picture that is sharp all the way through. This page explains how — and how the five methods on the menu differ. No background required.

The problem

A camera lens can only focus at one distance at a time. Point it at something close and the background goes soft; focus on the background and the near object goes soft. For a landscape that hardly matters. For a close-up of an insect, a coin or a circuit board, it means no single photo can show the whole subject sharply.

The way round it is to take several photos, moving the focus a little each time, and then combine them — keeping, from each one, only the parts that came out sharp.

The recipe they all follow

Every method here does the same four things. They differ only in how they do the middle two — and that is where all the trade-offs come from.

The hard part is "decide". Sharpness has to be judged from the picture itself, with no knowledge of the scene. The usual clue is that a sharp region changes a lot from one pixel to the next, while a blurred one changes smoothly. That clue is good but not perfect — film grain also changes a lot from pixel to pixel, which is why noisy photos confuse some methods.

Pixel by pixel, or block by block?

The single biggest difference between the classical methods is how finely they decide. Judging each pixel separately follows an outline exactly but is easily fooled by noise; judging a whole square at a time is steadier but leaves visible steps along a diagonal.

The methods, one at a time

Guided Filter

pixel by pixel

It starts by splitting each frame in two: a base layer holding the broad shapes and colour, and a detail layer holding the fine texture. Sharpness lives entirely in the detail layer, so that is where the comparison happens.

Comparing pixel by pixel gives a speckled, unreliable first guess — a single pixel is thin evidence. So the guess is smoothed. The clever part is that the smoothing is guided by the photo itself: it spreads decisions freely across a flat area, but refuses to spread them across a visible edge. Decisions therefore stop exactly where objects stop, instead of bleeding into the background.

GFG-FGF

pixel by pixel, with a shortlist

Closely related to the guided filter, and faster. It measures how much each small neighbourhood stands out from its surroundings, then refines the result the same edge-aware way.

It adds one step the others do not have: before looking at any detail, it gives each frame a single overall sharpness score, and drops any frame scoring under 15% of the best one. The idea is to save time by ignoring hopelessly blurred frames.

DCT

block by block

This one chops each frame into small squares — eight pixels across by default — and asks a simple question of each: how much do the pixels inside it differ from one another? A sharp square contains strong contrast; a blurred square is comparatively uniform. The square with the most variation wins.

Working on squares makes it by far the fastest method, and it needs no special libraries. The cost is that every decision lands on a grid, so a diagonal boundary between near and far comes out slightly stepped. A median filter cleans up isolated mistakes afterwards, which helps — though it also rounds off details genuinely smaller than a block.

Its weak spot is grain: noise looks exactly like "pixels differing from one another", so on a high-ISO shot it can rate a noisy blurred square above a clean sharp one.

DTCWT

coarse to fine

Rather than choosing one scale to work at, this method looks at the picture at several scales at once — first the finest texture, then progressively larger structures — and in several directions at each scale.

Because it can see both a thin line and the large soft shape it sits on, its decisions near a boundary between near and far are better informed than a method judging one small neighbourhood at a time. In our measurements it produces the cleanest edges of any non-neural method. The price is time: every extra level is more work.

StackMFF-V4

learned

Instead of a rule written by a person, this one was shown thousands of focus stacks during training along with the right answer for each, and adjusted itself until it could produce those answers. Nobody told it what sharpness looks like; it worked that out from examples.

That is why it usually wins: recognising which frame is in focus is exactly the kind of judgement that is awkward to write as a formula but straightforward to learn from examples. The catch is the flip side - it is only reliable on the sort of pictures it was trained on. Ordinary photography is well covered; microscopy, medical imaging and other specialist material are not.

IFCNN Refine

optional extra pass

This is not a fusion method - it is a second pass that runs after one. It takes the fused picture plus your original frames, and looks for detail the first pass lost, copying it back from whichever frame actually had it.

The middle picture below is the guided filter's result after refining; on its own it looks much the same. The third picture is the difference between the two, multiplied six times. Notice the change is not confined to the edges it was meant to repair - it covers the whole frame. That spread is a colour shift, and it is the reason this stage scores below the plain guided filter in our measurements.

So which should you pick?

For ordinary photography, StackMFF-V4 needs no tuning and usually wins. If you want something predictable and hand-written, DTCWT was the strongest of those in our tests, with Guided Filter close behind and quicker. DCT is the one to reach for when speed matters more than perfect edges — but not on a noisy photo.
