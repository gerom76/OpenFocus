The Depth Map method:
how it decides, pixel by pixel

Every other classical method in OpenFocus decides in a transform domain — a pyramid band, a DCT block, a wavelet coefficient. The depth-map family decides in the image plane, from a local contrast measure, and it is the only one that names an answer a human can look at: which frame is sharpest at each pixel. That map is the method, and both of its selection rules read it.

Pages 2–4 are the mechanism as it ships, in the order the pixels move through it. Pages 5–6 set it against Helicon Focus, whose Methods A and B are the same two selection rules and whose two dials sit in the same two places — the comparison the method is most often asked to survive.

The pipeline end to end

Why decide in the image plane

A pyramid or wavelet decides on coefficients, and a sum of coefficients taken from different frames can land on a value that appears in none of them — the invented pixels the pyramid needs an envelope clamp to prevent. Deciding on pixels cannot do that. The price is that a single window has to stand in for the multiscale prior a transform gets for free, which is what steps 4–6 are about. It is also what makes Max auditable: every output pixel traces back to one frame and one coordinate.

Why the map is the point

Max produces an explicit statement — frame k is sharpest at this pixel — which is a range estimate, not just an intermediate. It is what drives 3D, anaglyph and relief output in the tools that expose it, and four of five competitors write it to a file. OpenFocus builds it every render and discards it; see page 6. Average produces no map at all — that is the trade it makes for its noise gain.

The two dials, in one line each

Kernel — how much neighbourhood a frame has to win over before it is believed. Small follows fine detail and is noisier; large is steadier and reaches further past an edge.
Halo suppression — how far a sharply focused region may claim beyond its own outline. Default 0; reach for it only if a glow survives the measure.
Not dials: the σ 0.6 prefilter and the 3 × 5×5 despeckle are constants. The second of those is the one control Helicon has and OpenFocus does not — page 6.

Stage 2 of 4 · the focus measure

What "sharpest here" is actually measured as

One number per pixel per frame — and the whole method's quality is decided by it

The chain, per frame

  1. Smooth it — for measurement only. cv2.GaussianBlur(img, (7,7), 0.6) A bare 3-tap Laplacian is the most noise-sensitive high-pass there is, and on flat regions the decision was ranking frames by their noise floor. The blurred copy is scratch; output pixels are still taken from the untouched frames, so it costs nothing in resolution.
  2. Take the Laplacian, per channel. cv2.Laplacian(img, CV_32F, ksize=3) OpenCV's ksize=3 is the Sobel-derived [[2,0,2],[0,-8,0],[2,0,2]] stencil, not the four-neighbour one — the GPU path reproduces those exact taps.
  3. Square, then sum across channels. E = Σc Lc² Summing before the decision is what ties a pixel's three channels to one frame. Decide per channel instead and a pixel's red can come from one slice and its blue from another — a colour present in no source. That was DTCWT's bug for years.
  4. Pool it over the dial's window. wide = boxFilter(E, k×k) A bare |∇²| is zero on flat-but-in-focus areas and noisy everywhere else. Pooling turns it into a region measure, so selection follows genuinely sharp regions instead of speckling.
  5. Pool it again, narrowly. near = boxFilter(E, (k÷3)×(k÷3)) Local evidence that barely leaks past an occlusion — see the figure.
  6. Combine as a geometric mean. F = √(max(wide · near, 0)) A frame has to carry both to win. Where the energy really is uniform over the window the two poolings agree and this is exactly the plain pooled energy, so flat regions decide as they always did. The clamp is not cosmetic: boxFilter accumulates a running sum and can leave a tiny negative across a sharp edge, and a NaN out of sqrt loses every > it is compared with.
  7. Optionally, grey-dilate by the halo radius. cv2.dilate(F, ellipse(r)) · r = 0 by default Lets a strongly focused region claim the band its defocused glow contaminates in the other frames. It does not remove a ring so much as fill one with defocused pixels, which is why it is off by default.

Why two windows and not one

Frame holding the foreground contourFrame holding the background texturePooled focus energy, each panel scaled to its own peak · kernel 51, narrow window 17

A 1-D slice across an occlusion. A box window is edge-blind, so one pooling smears the contour’s enormous energy 51 // 2 px in every direction — including out over background belonging to another slice, which is then taken from a frame where it is defocused. The narrow window barely leaks, so requiring a frame to win on both collapses the band from 21 px to 7.

The narrow window is not always on. It is dropped below 5 px — a window that small hardly leaks, while a 3 px pooling of a squared Laplacian is mostly sensor noise, and letting noise into the decision costs more than the leak it would save. Because it is k ÷ 3, that puts the switch-on at kernel 15: at the default 9 the measure is the plain pooled energy it has always been, and the correction engages exactly where the ring becomes wide enough to see.

One measure, two ways to spend it

Both read the same energy maps; they differ only in what they do with a tie
A

Max — the hard per-pixel select

cf. Helicon Method B, Zerene DMap
index[y,x] = argmaxk Fk[y,x] strict >, walked in index order index = median5×5³( index ) before a single pixel is gathered out[y,x] = frame[ index[y,x] ][y,x] at the stack's own depth
  • The reduction records the winner, not the pixel. An index map costs one byte per pixel against twelve for a float32 BGR accumulator, and lets the result be gathered at native depth with no float round trip. The map widens to uint16 past 256 frames and uint32 past 65 536, so a deep stack cannot wrap.
  • A frame that wins nowhere is never loaded. The gather walks the frames once and skips any whose index never appears.

Why the map is despeckled first

The argmax carries no spatial prior whatsoever. Over a region no frame ever resolves — a background behind the focus sweep, a dark flat patch — every frame's energy sits at the same defocused level and the winner is decided by whatever noise survived the pooling. Neighbouring pixels then take frames from opposite ends of the stack, which render that background at visibly different blur and brightness. A depth map is piecewise-smooth: it varies continuously across a surface and steps only at an occlusion, so those pixels are outliers against their own neighbourhood — exactly what a median removes while leaving genuine steps standing.

Three narrow passes, not one wide median. Iterating a narrow median converges toward its root signal — removing what is smaller than the window without eroding what is larger — where one wide median rounds off real depth features of its own size too. Three 5×5 passes match a single true 9×9 on incoherence while keeping more in-focus detail, and 5 is also the widest window cv2.medianBlur takes above uint8: the exact 9×9 is a scipy call costing ~1 s on a 1.7 MP map against 15 ms for three cv2 passes. Measured cost on real stacks: mean PSNR over seven scenarios rose 41.87 → 42.27 dB at kernel 9 and SSIM 0.969 → 0.982; the one real loss is long_stack at kernel 51, −0.78 dB, and splitting the extra error by row puts 100.5% of it within 4 px of a depth-band boundary.

B

Average — the contrast-weighted blend

cf. Helicon Method A, Shine Stacker average mode
b = max( 0.1 · mean(F) , 1e-8 ) the baseline weight Σk Fk·Ik + b · Σk Ik out = ─────────────────────────── Σk Fk + b · N
  • There is no index map, so there is nothing to despeckle — the blend is coherent by construction, which is the mode's whole argument.
  • The baseline is scale-free. It tracks a fraction of the stack's own mean energy rather than an absolute level, so it means the same thing on a dark scene as on a bright one. The 1e-8 floor is only so a perfectly flat stack still averages instead of dividing by zero.
  • This is where the √N comes from. Where every frame is equally defocused the weights flatten and the region collapses to the plain mean of N frames — a free √N noise reduction that every hard-select method throws away by keeping one frame and discarding the rest.

What the baseline actually buys

With the baseline — as shippedPure contrast weighting

Twelve frames over a region none of them resolves, where grain alone leaves one frame’s energy at 3× the others’. Pure contrast weighting hands that frame 21.4% of the output no matter how meaningless the energy is — it is ranking noise. The baseline is a fixed fraction of the stack’s mean energy, so below it the weights flatten to 8.3% each and the region becomes the plain mean; above it the sharp frame still takes over.

Which mode to reach for. Max keeps colour and noise strictly inside one slice and invents nothing, so it is the honest choice on textured subjects and the only one that produces a depth map at all. Average is the answer where the stack is noisy or the subject is smooth — it is the only method in OpenFocus that turns stack depth into signal-to-noise rather than only into depth of field. Neither dominates, which is why the mature tools ship both and teach users to retouch between them.

Stage 4 of 4 · the implementation

What the code guarantees that the mathematics does not

Determinism, memory, device parity, depth — the properties a render is judged on when it is repeated

Determinism & frame order

  • The result does not depend on the order frames are given in. The argmax is taken over all frames at once, not folded pairwise, so reversing the stack returns a bit-identical image. This is a real distinction: it is what DTCWT lacked until 1.30.13, and it is what Helicon's own documentation says Method B cannot offer.
  • Ties break on the lowest index, always. Frames are measured on a pool but reduced in index order with a strict >, so two renders of the same stack are bitwise equal however the threads happened to be scheduled.
  • MODE_AVERAGE accumulates in index order for the same reason — float addition is not associative, and as_completed ordering would make repeat renders differ by about a level.

Memory, and why it is bounded

  • The stack is never held as float. Frames are normalised on demand, so only the handful in flight cost 12 bytes a pixel; one 60 MP frame is 720 MB as float32.
  • The pool is sized by memory, not only by threads. The count starts at min(8, cpu_count) — the OpenCV filters underneath are already internally parallel — then is capped at 25% of the free physical memory divided by an estimate of what one in-flight measurement actually holds. It only ever lowers the count, and says so once when it does.
  • Peak measured on 12 frames of 4000×3000 at kernel 9: +2.6 GB for Max, +2.8 GB for Average — down from 3.7 and 3.6 before the index map replaced the float accumulator.

Bit depth, end to end

  • An 8-bit stack returns uint8; a 16-bit stack returns uint16. A mixed stack is rescaled once to the common depth and stays there.
  • In Max mode the output is a gather, not a cast. Pixels are copied from the native-depth frames after the decision, so no float round trip stands between a source pixel and the file — every output value is exactly some input value.
  • In Average mode the blend is computed in float32 and converted once, with rounding rather than truncation.

  • The batch is derived, not pinned. Frames per chunk come from what cuda.mem_get_info reports free against an estimate of a frame's device cost — 15 for a 12 MP stack on a 16 GB card, 6 for 24 MP with halo suppression on — and the chunk halves and retries on OutOfMemoryError, so being wrong is slow rather than fatal.
  • The tie-break survives batching. The measurement is batched but the reduction still walks a chunk one frame at a time, because the strict > in index order is what makes both paths pick the same frame. A batched argmax would quietly resolve those the other way.
  • The halo element is reproduced, not approximated. max_pool2d gives a square window, which would push the band out to r·√2 at the corners and make the radius mean something different on each path. The ellipse is decomposed into one horizontal maximum per distinct row half-width, reproducing OpenCV's round(√(r²−dy²)) spans exactly — asserted bitwise equal to cv2.dilate for radii 1, 2, 4 and 8.
  • The median runs on the host, over a plane that is a couple of megabytes, so both paths go through the same cv2 call and agree on it exactly; only the pixels it actually moved are re-gathered, and only the chunks holding a frame those pixels now name are re-uploaded.
  • Two deliberate departures remain, both at the frame border: cv2 pools with BORDER_REFLECT where torch's reflect padding is BORDER_REFLECT_101, and Average sums a chunk as a batch rather than frame by frame. Inside the frame the two agree to better than 55 dB; Max is bitwise identical.

What is still missing — read this before page 5

  • The depth map is computed and discarded. This method literally builds the artefact four of five competing tools export as a file, and the substrate for 3D and anaglyph output. It cannot be saved.
  • The despeckle is not a dial. Three passes of 5×5 is a fixed constant, chosen on the fixtures. Helicon exposes precisely this as Smoothing, and it is the one parameter where the field has a control OpenFocus does not.
  • There is no contrast or trust threshold. The argmax is taken unconditionally everywhere, including where the measure is pure noise. The median that follows makes the speckle coherent — it does not make the estimate true. Zerene marks such regions and stretches the estimate across them instead.
  • No photometric normalisation upstream. The focus measure is contrast-linear, so a frame 5% brighter wins the argmax on identical detail. This is not a defect of this method — it corrupts the input to all of them — but the depth map is where it shows first, as a band of the map jumping to whichever frame the flash favoured.
  • No confidence is surfaced. best − second is available for free at the moment of the decision and is thrown away. "No frame is sharp here" is the most common cause of a disappointing render and is currently invisible.

There is a second, happier consequence: at kernel 17 the two-scale measure is on (page 2), so the Helicon-equivalent setting is also the regime where OpenFocus's occlusion correction is doing work.

What each tool is actually optimising for

Helicon's two dials describe a workflow: look wider until the halo goes away, then smooth until the seams do. Both are blunt and both cost detail, and the product's answer to what remains is not a better measure — it is the retouching brush, which copies a region from any chosen source frame into the result. The vendor's own documentation treats that as expected practice rather than as a failure.

OpenFocus has no retouching brush, so it cannot make that trade. Its answer to the same two artefacts is instead structural, and made once at the measure: the two-scale geometric mean removes the halo by asking a frame to prove the energy is local rather than by widening the window, and the median on the index map removes the seams without touching the pixels, so the fix cannot blur anything that was already right. That is why the despeckle is a constant and not a slider — but it is also why not having the slider is a real gap rather than a philosophical one: when the constant is wrong for a stack, there is nothing the user can do about it.

What to take from Helicon, in order

  1. Expose the despeckle. Helicon's Smoothing and OpenFocus's three median passes do the same job; one is a slider and one is a constant. The mechanism already exists and is shared by both device paths — this is a parameter, a UI row and a range, not an algorithm. It is the smallest item here, and the only place a competitor has a control OpenFocus lacks inside the method.
  2. Write the depth map out. It is built every render and thrown away. Four of five competing tools export it, and it is the substrate for the 3D output Helicon builds on top of it.
  3. Normalise brightness before measuring. Not this method's bug, but this method's map is where it shows first, and it is the one fix that improves every fusion method at once.
  4. Give the argmax a trust threshold. The median makes an unresolvable region coherent; it does not make it right. Marking those regions is what Zerene does, and the confidence signal it needs is already computed and discarded.

The honest summary

On the decision itself, OpenFocus is ahead. Its measure is occlusion-aware where the field's is a single window; its halo control is direct where the field's is a side effect of the analysis radius; its result does not depend on the order frames arrive in, which Helicon's Method B explicitly does; and it is reproducible bit for bit across runs and across devices, which nobody in the comparison claims. Every one of those is measured in docs/ALGORITHM_IMPROVEMENTS.md and guarded by tests.

On everything around the decision, Helicon is ahead — and by more than the list above suggests, because the items compound. Helicon's user can smooth the map when the default is wrong, save the map when they want it, retouch what neither method got right, and feed the whole thing properly exposed, dust-free RAW frames. The OpenFocus user gets a better decision and then has to accept it exactly as rendered.

That is the same shape the wider audit found across all eight methods: more algorithm than anyone, less pipeline than anyone. For this method specifically the correction is unusually cheap — exposing one constant and saving one array that is already in memory would close the two gaps that sit inside the method's own boundary.
