How a RAW becomes a DNG

A fused stack saved as DNG will not always look like the RAW it came from, and the settings that decide this are not obvious from their names. This page follows the pixels from the sensor to the file, and shows exactly what each option changes — including the two ways it can go wrong.

Two different kinds of file

A NEF and an OpenFocus DNG are both called “raw”, but they hold different things. The NEF holds the sensor's own mosaic — one colour measurement per pixel, untouched. Adobe's DNG Converter repackages exactly that, which is why a converted NEF behaves identically to the original and accepts any camera profile.

A fused stack cannot be that file. Focus stacking compares frames pixel by pixel, so it needs finished pixels: the mosaic has to be demosaiced, white balanced and developed before any of it can happen. By the time there is a result to save, the sensor data is several irreversible steps behind.

This is the root of every difference on this page. The DNG holds a picture that has already been rendered once. Everything the writer does is about stopping a converter rendering it a second time — or, if you ask for it, about putting the pixels back where a camera profile can find them.

Discrepancy 1: the transfer function

A raw converter assumes the numbers it reads are scene-linear — proportional to light. The pipeline's frames are display-referred: a curve has already been applied so they look right on a screen. Something has to reconcile the two.

DNG has a tag for saying so, LinearizationTable, and OpenFocus used it: keep the samples as they are, and describe the curve. It is the tidier answer and it costs nothing in the shadows. It is also, in practice, not always read. Luminar Neo ignores the tag, takes the encoded samples for light and applies its own curve on top.

The fix: apply it, don't declare it

The samples are now put through the curve on the way out, so the file really is scene-linear and there is no tag left to ignore. A converter cannot skip an encoding that is not there.

The cost is precision in the deep shadows, where linear light has few codes to spare, so samples are always written at 16 bits — an 8-bit frame is promoted, and the file is larger for it. Reading one back undoes the curve, which is exact everywhere the curve is steep enough to separate its inputs and within a couple of parts in 65535 at the very bottom.

The one exception is the lossy mode, which keeps the table. DNG restricts lossy to 8-bit samples, and 8 bits of linear light collapses the whole shadow half of the range into two or three values. It costs nothing there: any reader that can open a lossy DNG supports the tag, because Adobe's own lossy files are built the same way.

Discrepancy 2: the colour space

The second difference is the one people notice in the profile menu. A camera profile — “Nikon Z 6 2 Adobe Standard” and its relatives — exists to turn that sensor's RGB into a picture. Handed sRGB instead, it does its job perfectly and produces nonsense.

Camera space takes the pixels sRGB → XYZ → the camera's own coordinates, labels the file with that camera and its measured matrix, and sets AsShotNeutral to where daylight lands in those coordinates. The file becomes a synthetic RAW of the body the stack was shot on.

Nothing is lost in the transform

A camera's gamut contains sRGB's with room to spare, so every colour in the frame has a camera coordinate and nothing clips in either direction. Measured across saturated primaries, secondaries, neutrals and mixed tones, the round trip is exact to within a handful of parts in 65535.

What it costs is the default rendering. The file is a camera RAW now, so a converter develops it with that camera's profile and tone curve. It will look like the RAW developed in that converter — not like the fused result sitting beside it. The two modes answer different questions and only one can be answered at a time, which is why sRGB stays the default.

The neutral is the tell. 1, 1, 1 says the samples are already balanced and need nothing done to them. The camera-space triple says where daylight falls on that sensor, and a converter divides by it — which is exactly the step that makes a camera profile work.

A camera-space file also stops being our file. It names the camera, so OpenFocus no longer recognises it as its own output and reloads it by developing it, the same as any other RAW. An sRGB file reloads as the frame that was saved.

Discrepancy 3: compression

Independent of colour, and a smaller decision than it looks. Sizes below are the same frame written three ways and read back.

Uncompressed is the default: the strips are the pixels, nothing to go wrong, fastest to write. Lossless stores prediction errors rather than approximations, so it gives back the same frame for a smaller file, at the cost of a slower save and a dependency — how much smaller depends entirely on the picture, and the demo frame above is noisier than a real photograph, so a real stack usually does better than the figure shown. Lossy is a proxy format: 8-bit only, so a 16-bit result is narrowed on the way out, and it is the one mode that still declares its transfer function rather than applying it.

Which to choose

sRGB

default

Choose it when the DNG should look like what OpenFocus produced — when you are handing the fused result to someone, archiving it, or matching it against a JPEG XL or TIFF written from the same pixels.

Renders as the fused result.

Do not pick a camera profile for it. The menu will offer them; they are wrong for this file and no tag can stop you.

Camera colour space

opt-in

Choose it when you want to finish the picture in Lightroom or Luminar with that camera's profiles and rendering, treating the fused stack as though it were a single exposure off the body.

Camera profiles behave.

Needs the source RAW — the colour matrix lives in LibRaw, not in any EXIF block. A stack fused from JPEGs, a monochrome result or the lossy mode falls back to sRGB and says so in the log.
